Part 1: The Jurisprudential Foundation of the Right to Travel

The right of a citizen to travel upon the public highways and to transport property thereon, either by carriage or by automobile, is an inalienable Common Law right protected under the United States Constitution. It is not a state-granted privilege, nor does it require a commercial driver's license when conducted outside of commercial transport for hire.

In Corfield v. Coryell, 6 F. Cas. 546 (C.C.E.D. Pa. 1823), Justice Bushrod Washington established the privileges-and-immunities doctrine, declaring that all citizens hold "the right… to pass through, or to reside in any other state, for purposes of trade, agriculture, professional pursuits, or otherwise." In Slaughter-House Cases, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36 (1873), and reaffirmed in Saenz v. Roe, 526 U.S. 489 (1999), the Supreme Court cemented the three components of constitutional travel: (1) the right to enter and leave states freely; (2) the right to be treated as a welcome visitor; and (3) the right to establish residence without penalty.

The vehicle regulatory seam is governed by Hendrick v. Maryland, 235 U.S. 610 (1915), which affirms that states may regulate commercial transportation and public highway safety. However, the term "Driver" in statutory motor vehicle codes is historically and strictly defined as one who is "employed in the business of conducting a conveyance for commercial compensation." When traveling privately, you are an unregistered traveler exercising a common law right.

1. HISTORICAL FOUNDATIONS

▪Articles of Confederation, art. IV (1781): "the people of different States… shall have free ingress and regress to and from any other State" (near-verbatim) — the first American statement of the right.
▪***Corfield v. Coryell*, 6 F. Cas. 546 (C.C.E.D. Pa. 1823) (Bushrod Washington, J.)** [K]: the privileges-and-immunities canon — citizens hold "the right… to pass through, or to reside in any other state, for purposes of trade, agriculture, professional pursuits, or otherwise." The root authority of the entire doctrine.
▪***The Slaughter-House Cases*, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36 (1873):** confined the federal privileges-or-immunities clause but left movement protection standing in multiple constitutional homes [K].

2. THE MODERN CONSTITUTIONAL DOCTRINE — THREE BRANCHES (*Saenz v. Roe*, 526 U.S. 489 (1999) [K])

1. Enter and leave a state — *Crandall v. Nevada*, 73 U.S. 35 (1868) (no per-capita transit tax) [K]; *Edwards v. California*, 314 U.S. 160 (1941) (no migration bars) [K].

2. Be treated as a welcome visitor — nondiscrimination against nonresidents (art. IV, § 2) [K].

3. Settle as a new resident — *Shapiro v. Thompson*, 394 U.S. 618 (1969) (one-year welfare-residence rule invalid) [K]; *Memorial Hospital v. Maricopa County*, 415 U.S. 250 (1974) (residence-based medical benefits invalid) [K]; *Vlandis v. Kline*, 412 U.S. 441 (1973) (tuition status) [K].

▪International travel: *Kent v. Dulles*, 357 U.S. 116 (1958) (passport denial requires due process); *Aptheker v. Secretary of State*, 378 U.S. 500 (1964) (subversive-travel ban void); *Haig v. Agee*, 453 U.S. 280 (1981) (passport revocation permitted on national-security grounds) [K].
▪Private/criminal interference: *United States v. Guest*, 383 U.S. 745 (1966) (federal prosecution for conspiracies to interfere with interstate travel); *Griffin v. Breckenridge*, 403 U.S. 88 (1971) (Thirteenth Amendment reaches private interference) [K].

3. THE VEHICLE-REGULATION SEAM — WHERE "TRAVEL" MEETS "DRIVING"

▪***Hendrick v. Maryland*, 235 U.S. 610 (1915)** [V]: unanimous — "A State may require registration of motor vehicles; and a reasonable license fee is not unconstitutional." *Kane v. New Jersey*, 242 U.S. 160 (1916) [K] — registration/fees upheld.
▪Due process on the license itself: *Bell v. Burson*, 402 U.S. 535 (1971) (suspension after an accident requires a hearing — the license is a property interest); *Mackey v. Montrym*, 443 U.S. 1 (1979) (implied-consent immediate suspension upheld) [K].
▪What states require to operate on public roads (pattern [K]): tested driver's license; registration + title; insurance/financial responsibility (nearly universal); equipment codes.
▪The holdings converge: regulation is constitutional; the *process* around it must be fair. That is the whole seam.

1. The constitutional right to travel is real — here is what it protects

| Authority | What it establishes |

| Art. IV, § 2 (Privileges and Immunities) | Citizens of each state get other states' privileges [K] |

Scope: movement between and within states, by any means — walking, bicycling, common carriers, driving *with a valid license*. It has never been held to exempt anyone from motor-vehicle regulation.

2. Licensing is constitutional — the controlling Supreme Court authority

▪***Hendrick v. Maryland*, 235 U.S. 610 (1915)** [V]: unanimous (9–0). Holding (per the U.S. Reports text): "A State may require registration of motor vehicles; and a reasonable license fee is not unconstitutional as denial of equal protection." The Court upheld Maryland's motor-vehicle law as a valid police-power and revenue measure.
▪*Kane v. New Jersey*, 242 U.S. 160 (1916): registration/fee schemes further upheld [K].
▪The Supreme Court has never wavered; every state appellate court to address the "traveler vs. driver" distinction has rejected it [V — documented in the Wikipedia sovereign-citizen survey, the UNC School of Government guide (NC Court of Appeals rejections), and a 2022 Kansas federal decision calling repeated right-to-travel claims frivolous].

3. What the "traveler" theory costs in practice

Traffic citations that escalate; vehicle impoundment and towing fees; insurance violations (separate offense in most states); license suspensions that compound; contempt exposure when the theory is litigated repeatedly; and — where false instruments accompany it — the fraud and false-lien exposure in the knowledge base's file 8. No court in the United States has accepted the theory; the FBI and UNC materials document it as a recognized pattern [V].

4. The narrow, real accommodations

▪Hardship/occupational licenses — nearly all states issue restricted licenses after suspension for work, school, or medical needs (state DMV law [K]).
▪Religious objections — the *Quaring* (1985) photo-ID line produced occasional exemptions, but *Smith* (1990) made neutral requirements presumptively valid; REAL ID standards now govern federal-use eligibility [V/K].
▪Off-road/private property — vehicle regulation does not reach private land; that is the legitimate core of the "freedom of movement" instinct behind the theory [K].

5. Stage A–F classification

B (support): none in any controlling authority. C (contrary): *Hendrick* (1915) [V]; uniform state-court rejection [V]. D: Rejected by controlling courts. E: verified this session. VOLUME IV — SEPARATE COURTS AND JUDICIAL JURISDICTION

1. Executive Overview

The United States has many courts, but they all trace to identifiable constitutional or statutory authority, publish their decisions, and apply the Constitution. The architecture:

1. Article III courts (life-tenured judges): 94 district courts; 13 courts of appeals (12 regional + Federal Circuit); the Supreme Court.

2. Article I legislative courts (Congress creates under enumerated powers).

3. Article IV territorial courts.

4. State courts (sovereigns under their own constitutions).

5. Tribal courts (inherent sovereignty).

6. Administrative adjudicators (executive power).

The theory that any of these is a "secret" or "parallel" system without constitutional constraint has no support in any authority — and the doctrine of *strictly limited* subject-matter jurisdiction is the opposite of secret: it is enforced by courts on their own motion.

2. Article III Courts

Created under Art. III (life tenure, salary protection — *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803)); *Martin v. Hunter's Lessee*, 14 U.S. (1 Wheat.) 304 (1816) (federal appellate power over state courts). Jurisdiction is statutory today: federal question (28 U.S.C. § 1331), diversity (§ 1332, > $75,000, *Strawbridge v. Curtiss*, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 267 (1806) complete diversity), admiralty (§ 1333), plus specialty grants.

3. Article I Legislative Courts — BINDING

▪*American Insurance Co. v. Canter*, 26 U.S. (1 Pet.) 511 (1828): Congress may create territorial courts with non-Article III judges.
▪*Ex parte Bakelite Corp.*, 279 U.S. 438 (1929): categories of public-right disputes may go to legislative courts.
▪*Palmore v. United States*, 411 U.S. 389 (1973): D.C. local courts are constitutional.
▪Bankruptcy courts: *Northern Pipeline Constr. Co. v. Marathon Pipe Line Co.*, 458 U.S. 50 (1982) (Art. I bankruptcy judges cannot exercise ordinary Art. III jurisdiction over common-law suits); *Stern v. Marshall*, 564 U.S. 462 (2011); *Wellness Int'l Network v. Sharif*, 575 U.S. 665 (2015) (party consent permits).
▪U.S. Tax Court: *Freytag v. Commissioner*, 501 U.S. 868 (1991); *Kuretski v. Commissioner*, 575 U.S. 51 (2015).
▪Military: *Ex parte Milligan*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2 (1866); *Solorio v. United States*, 483 U.S. 435 (1987); U.S. Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces.

4. Administrative Tribunals After *Jarkesy* (2024)

SEC v. Jarkesy, 603 U.S. 109 (U.S. June 27, 2024): when the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud (common-law analog), the Seventh Amendment requires a jury trial in federal court. Effect: in-house administrative penalty adjudication is unconstitutional for such claims; scope beyond securities fraud is being litigated (FTC, FINRA, etc.). *Loper Bright* (June 28, 2024) simultaneously restored judicial independence on statutory meaning. Administrative adjudication of genuinely "public rights" (taxes, benefits, immigration) continues.

5. Tribal and Immigration Courts

▪Tribal courts: *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313 (1978) (retained sovereignty); *Duro*, *Lara*, *McGirt* (2020), *Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta* (2022), *Haaland v. Brackeen* (2023) — see Volume I § 2.6.
▪Immigration courts (EOIR, Dept. of Justice): executive adjudicators; IJ decisions subject to BIA review then courts of appeals (8 U.S.C. § 1252). Status: constitutional as exercises of executive power over removable aliens.

6. The Merger of Law and Equity

▪FRCP Rule 2 (1938): "There is one form of action — the civil action."
▪*Beacon Theatres, Inc. v. Westover*, 359 U.S. 500 (1959); *Dairy Queen, Inc. v. Wood*, 369 U.S. 469 (1962): legal issues get jury trial first.
▪*Curtis v. Loether*, 415 U.S. 189 (1974): damages actions trigger the Seventh Amendment; *Granfinanciera, S.A. v. Nordberg*, 492 U.S. 33 (1989).
▪Consequence for pseudolaw: the "law courts vs. equity courts" dichotomy no longer describes procedure; separate courts of chancery survive only in a few state names (e.g., Delaware Chancery — a specialized court, not a parallel system).

VOLUME IX — JURISDICTIONAL CHALLENGES AND PROCEDURAL LAW

1. Executive Overview

Jurisdiction is the court's lawful power to act; procedure is how it is tested. The rules are mechanical, strict, and mostly unforgiving of delay — and they are entirely different from the pseudolegal "non-consent" fantasy. This volume is the procedural field manual for Volume IV.

2. Never Waivable vs. Waivable

| Rule | Subject-matter jurisdiction | Personal jurisdiction | Venue | Immunities |

|---|---|---|---|---|

| Can be raised anytime? | Yes — court *must* dismiss, even on appeal (FRCP 12(h)(3)) | No — waived if not timely raised | No — forfeiture (28 U.S.C. § 1406; *Moore v. Beaulieu*? — general rule) | Yes/No by type (Eleventh Amendment: structural; FSIA: must be raised or forfeited? — courts split pre-*Vitol*) |

| Key authority | *Arbaugh v. Y&H Corp.*, 546 U.S. 500 (2006); *Henderson v. Shinseki*, 562 U.S. 428 (2011) | *Insurance Corp. of Ireland v. Note on FSIA: sovereign immunity "is not a mere procedural rule" — it strips jurisdiction (Kuwait Airways v. American Sec. Bank, 590 F.2d 1270 (D.C. Cir. 1978)).

3. The Lawful Challenge Sequence

1. Answer or Rule 12 motion deadline (21 days after service; waivable).

2. Rule 12(b) defenses: (1) lack of SMJ; (2) lack of PJ; (3) improper venue; (4) insufficient process; (5) insufficient service; (6) failure to state a claim; (7) failure to join a Rule 19 party.

3. Removal/remand: 28 U.S.C. §§ 1441–1453; § 1447(c) 30-day remand clock for defects other than SMJ.

4. Appeal: final-judgment rule (28 U.S.C. § 1291); collateral-order doctrine (*Cohen v. Beneficial Indus. Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541 (1949)).

5. Supreme Court: certiorari (28 U.S.C. § 1257) — discretionary.

4. Abstention and Restraint Doctrines

*Younger v. Harris*, 401 U.S. 37 (1971) (federal courts decline to enjoin pending state proceedings); *Pullman* (certification of state-law questions); *Burford* (complex state regulatory schemes); *Colorado River* (concurrent proceedings); *Rooker-Feldman*, as narrowed by *Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Saudi Basic Indus. Corp.*, 544 U.S. 280 (2005) — lower federal courts cannot review final state-court judgments; only the Supreme Court can.

6. Practical Checklist — A Jurisdictional Challenge That Will Actually Work

1. Identify the grant of jurisdiction (statute + article).

2. Test the *elements* (maritime facts? diversity complete? amount? well-pleaded complaint?).

3. Preserve personal/venue objections in the first responsive pleading.

4. Raise immunity (Eleventh Amendment, FSIA, tribal) early — correctly, by party and remedy type.

5. Never couple a valid challenge with pseudolegal declarations — they contaminate the record and invite sanctions.

1. SEC v. Jarkesy, 603 U.S. 109 (2024) — WHERE IT STANDS AND WHAT IT REACHES

Holding (6–3, Roberts, C.J.): When the SEC seeks civil monetary penalties for securities fraud — claims mirroring common-law fraud — the Seventh Amendment requires a jury trial in an Article III court. Congress cannot channel such claims to in-house administrative adjudication before ALJs.

Foundational logic: (1) the claims mirror common-law fraud; (2) no meaningful "public rights" exception applies to such private-rights analogues; (3) a statutory scheme's expansion of agency power cannot erode the jury right.

What it does NOT do: it does not abolish administrative adjudication. Public-rights matters (customs, immigration, benefits, tax deficiency proceedings, licensing) remain. *Jarkesy* is a targeted, jury-based limit — precisely the opposite of the "agencies are unconstitutional" claim popular in movement material.

Current tracking (verified): the Congressional Research Service's survey of appellate application — *Two Years After SEC v. Jarkesy: Overview of Selected Appellate Decisions* (CRS LSB11473, Sept. 2, 2026; Shedd & Shen, 200 pp.) — collects the two-year wave of circuit decisions applying *Jarkesy*'s framework to SEC, CFTC, and other agency penalty structures. The report exists and is the canonical starting point; the 200-page detail is the next expansion target of this knowledge base.

Practical significance for litigants: where an agency seeks penalties on common-law-fraud-like claims, the demand for an Article III forum with a jury is now a *legitimate, winning* structural argument — an example of how genuine procedural challenges work, in contrast to status-based ones that never have.

2. Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo, 603 U.S. 369 (2024) — IN PRACTICE

Holding (6–3, Roberts, C.J.): The APA "requires courts to exercise their independent judgment in deciding whether an agency has acted within its statutory authority." Chevron deference is overruled. Courts may still seek "subsidiary regulatory determinations" and respect agency views — the revival of Skidmore v. Swift & Co., 323 U.S. 134 (1944) weight, not binding deference.

Where practice stands (verified through mid-2026):

▪SCOTUSblog's one-year review (Oct. 2025) documents the shift toward textualism and a re-emergent major-questions discipline in agency cases.
▪Empirical work (Minn. L. Rev. 2025, Craig) and practice surveys (Benesch, Aug. 2025) find courts differing on how much Skidmore weight agency positions get — some discount heavily, some cite agency reasoning with respect.
▪A mid-2026 empirical survey (progressivereform.org, June 10, 2026) measures Skidmore respect applied in roughly 31% of relevant cases, with no appreciable systemic impact on outcomes yet — courts are using independent judgment without a uniform weight formula.
▪The Bipartisan Policy Center's two-year explainer confirms no Supreme Court retrenchment as of 2026.

Practical significance: statutory-interpretation arguments against agency readings are now *counsel arguments, not deference losses* — a genuine, current avenue of procedural challenge.

3. FALSE-LIEN AND PAPER-RETALIATION LAW (FEDERAL + STATE PATTERN)

Federal: 18 U.S.C. § 1521 — filing a false lien or encumbrance against a federal judge or federal law-enforcement official (or their property) "in retaliation" for official acts: fine or imprisonment up to 10 years, or both (verified, Cornell LII). The DOJ Criminal Tax Manual documents § 1521's use against tax-defier and sovereign-citizen instruments ("Claim of Injury," "Notice of Lien" variants) (verified).

▪California: the 2015–16 session's AB 1267 analysis documents sovereign-citizen false-lien filings against judges, lawyers, and IRS employees as the legislative record justifying relief (CA legislative record).

▪National posture: SPLC's November 13, 2025 investigative update ("paper terrorism") and HUD-OIG's scam advisory document the pattern's persistence and enforcement.
▪NASS 2014 survey remains the national baseline study of state responses to fraudulent UCC filings (verified).

Filings without one are: (a) void and expungeable; (b) actionable (slander of title); (c) criminal where retaliatory; (d) sanctions-and-filing-ban fodder in the litigant's own case.

A. SOVEREIGN IMMUNITY — FEDERAL SYSTEM

1. Chisholm v. Georgia, 2 U.S. (2 Dall.) 419 (U.S. 1793)

▪Facts: Executor of a South Carolina merchant sued Georgia in federal court on state bonds.
▪Issue: Is a state suable in federal court by a citizen of another state under art. III, § 2?
▪Holding: Yes — 4–1. Georgia was subject to jurisdiction.
▪Reasoning: The text of art. III permitted the suit; the Court read jurisdiction over "Controversies… between a State and Citizens of another State" literally.
▪Subsequent treatment: The reaction was immediate and constitutional — the Eleventh Amendment (ratified 1795) reversed the holding by amendment, the first use of the amendment power to overrule the Court.
▪Relevance: Root authority for state sovereign immunity; the sovereign-citizen literature's "state citizen" arguments reverse-engineer this history without the amendment that followed.

2. Hans v. Louisiana, 134 U.S. 1 (1890)

▪Facts: Citizen of Louisiana sued Louisiana on state bonds (federal diversity/Question grounds).
▪Issue: Does the Eleventh Amendment bar a state's own citizens from suing it in federal court?
▪Holding: Yes. State sovereign immunity extends beyond the Amendment's literal text.
▪Reasoning: The Amendment presupposes, and the Constitution presupposes, state immunity; the diversity-clause reading that would let a citizen sue his own state was not intended.
▪Subsequent treatment: Basis of the modern immunity doctrine; refined by *Seminole Tribe* and *Alden*.
▪Relevance: Closes the "own citizen" loophole — which is why modern "state national" immunity arguments have no textual or doctrinal foothold.

3. Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida, 517 U.S. 44 (1996)

▪Facts: Tribe sued Florida under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act's abrogation clause.
▪Issue: May Congress abrogate state sovereign immunity under the Commerce Clause power?
▪Holding: No — 5–4. Commerce Clause abrogation is invalid.
▪Reasoning: *Pennsylvania v. Union Gas* overruled; immunity is a constitutional condition of federalism; Congress's Article I powers are "the power to legislate, not to override."
▪Subsequent treatment: *Ex parte Young* (1908) prospective relief preserved; later applied in *Alden*.
▪Relevance: Immunity is constitutional, not statutory — cannot be argued around; equally binds everyone.

4. Alden v. Maine, 527 U.S. 706 (1999)

▪Facts: Maine state probation officers sued Maine in Maine courts under the federal FLSA.
▪Issue: Does state sovereign immunity bar federal-law suits in state courts?
▪Holding: Yes — 7–2.
▪Reasoning: The Eleventh Amendment "embraces" a broader immunity the states retained on entering the Union; Congress cannot conscript state courts as forums for damages actions against states.
▪Subsequent treatment: Central to the anti-commandeering line (*Printz*, *Murphy*).
▪Relevance: Immunity is a federalism doctrine — not a personal-status doctrine; no private person holds an analogous immunity from jurisdiction.

B. FOREIGN SOVEREIGN & DUAL SOVEREIGNTY

5. The Schooner Exchange v. McFaddon, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 116 (1812)

▪Facts: Private claimants seized a French warship in Philadelphia; the vessel's consul sought dismissal.
▪Issue: Does U.S. jurisdiction reach a foreign sovereign's warship?
▪Holding: No. Chief Justice Marshall: full territorial jurisdiction is subject to exceptions by consent or necessity; a foreign sovereign warship passes immune.
▪Subsequent treatment: Foundational of foreign sovereign immunity; codified by FSIA 1976.
▪Relevance: The actual origin of immunity-as-consent — the reverse of the "sovereign by declaration" idea.

6. Republic of Austria v. Altmann, 541 U.S. 677 (2004)

▪Facts: Nazi-era seizure of Klimt paintings; suit under FSIA's expropriation exception.
▪Issue: Does FSIA apply to pre-enactment conduct?
▪Holding: Yes — FSIA's jurisdictional rules apply retroactively; immunity is jurisdictional, not a vested right.
▪Relevance: FSIA exceptions (commercial activity, expropriation, terrorism) are the *only* routes around foreign-state immunity — a closed list.

7. Samantar v. Yousuf, 560 U.S. 305 (2010)

▪Facts: Tort suit against a former Somali official for torture.
▪Issue: Does the FSIA cover individual foreign officials?
▪Holding: No — FSIA governs "foreign state[s]"; individual immunity (if any) is common-law/diplomatic, decided case by case.
▪Relevance: Shows the layered, statute-specific structure of immunity; nothing here supports personal immunity claims by U.S. litigants.

8. Gamble v. United States, 587 U.S. 675 (2019)

▪Facts: State conviction for firearm possession followed by federal prosecution for the same act.
▪Issue: Does dual prosecution violate the Double Jeopardy Clause?
▪Holding: No — 6–3. Federal and state governments are separate sovereigns.
▪Reasoning: Each sovereign derives power from a distinct source; the Fifth Amendment bars successive prosecutions by the *same* sovereign.
▪Subsequent treatment: Longstanding doctrine reaffirmed; recent scholarship questions it but no Court movement.
▪Relevance: Establishes the vertical (not horizontal) structure of American sovereignty a litigant is subject to — simultaneously.

C. FEDERALISM AND ENUMERATED POWER

9. McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819)

▪Facts: Maryland taxed the Second Bank of the United States; bank cashier prosecuted for unpaid stamp tax.
▪Issue: (1) May Congress charter a bank absent express power? (2) May a state tax a federal instrumentality?
▪Holding: (1) Yes. (2) No — "the power to tax involves the power to destroy."
▪Reasoning: Necessary and Proper Clause grants "great" power to execute enumerated powers; supremacy bars state interference. Marshall: the government "emanates from the people… exercised in general, by a government… to which the whole society is brought."
▪Subsequent treatment: The core of implied-powers doctrine; never displaced.
▪Relevance: Disposes of "the Federal Reserve/banks are unconstitutional" claims — Congress's banking and monetary powers are settled (*Knox v. Lee*, *Norman* below).

10. Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1 (1824)

▪Facts: New York-granted steamboat monopoly vs. federal coastal licensing.
▪Issue: What is "commerce among the several States," and does federal law displace state grants?
▪Holding: Commerce includes navigation and intercourse; federal license prevails.
▪Reasoning: "Commerce, undoubtedly, is traffic, but it is something more — it is intercourse."
▪Relevance: Root of the commerce-power arc through *Wickard* and its partial reversal in *Lopez*.

11. Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111 (1942)

▪Facts: Farmer fined under the Agricultural Adjustment Act for exceeding a wheat allotment grown for on-farm consumption.
▪Issue: May Congress regulate wheat never entering commerce?
▪Holding: Yes — the aggregation doctrine: home-consumed wheat, aggregated, affects the market.
▪Subsequent treatment: The high-water mark until *Lopez* (1995).
▪Relevance: Important against both extreme claims — that commerce power is unlimited (it is not, post-*Lopez*) and that it is narrow only (it reached home-grown wheat for 53 years).

12. United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549 (1995)

▪Facts: Gun-Free School Zones Act prosecution for carrying a pistol near a school.
▪Issue: Does the Commerce Clause reach mere possession of a gun in a school zone?
▪Holding: No — 5–4; first Commerce Clause limit since 1937.
▪Reasoning: Three categories of commerce power (channels; instrumentalities; activities with substantial relation); possession of a gun is none of them; the aggregation of *Wickard* involved economic activity — gun possession is not.
▪Subsequent treatment: *Morrison*; *Raich* (2005) upheld federal power over home-grown medical marijuana as economic.
▪Relevance: Federal power is real but enumerated — the accurate answer to both absolutist and nullificationist claims.

13. United States v. Morrison, 529 U.S. 598 (2000)

▪Facts: VAWA civil remedy for an alleged assault at Virginia Tech.
▪Issue: May Congress create a federal civil remedy for gender-motivated violence?
▪Holding: No — not economic activity; § 5 power requires congruence and proportionality (*Boerne*).
▪Relevance: States retain the police power; the Fourteenth Amendment directs federal power *against state action*.

14. City of Boerne v. Flores, 521 U.S. 507 (1997)

▪Facts: RFRA claimed to override a local zoning decision against a church expansion.
▪Issue: May Congress "define" the scope of constitutional rights under § 5?
▪Holding: No — § 5 is remedial, not definitional; RFRA invalid as applied to the states.
▪Relevance: The separation-of-powers rule that stops statutes from redefining constitutional rights — including by "religious" or "ecclesiastical" instruments claiming to displace secular jurisdiction.

15. NFIB v. Sebelius, 567 U.S. 519 (2012)

▪Facts: Challenge to the Affordable Care Act's individual mandate and Medicaid expansion.
▪Issue: Is the mandate valid under the Commerce Clause, and is the Medicaid expansion coercive?
▪Holding: Not commerce (unanimous on that point) but valid as a tax (5–4); Medicaid expansion unconstitutionally coercive in part.
▪Relevance: Confirms both limit and breadth of federal spending/commerce power — the balanced view.

D. MILITARY GOVERNMENT AND EMERGENCY POWERS

16. Ex parte Milligan, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2 (1866)

▪Facts: Civilian in Indiana tried and sentenced to death by military commission during the Civil War.
▪Issue: May a civilian be tried by military commission where civil courts are open?
▪Holding: No — unanimous on the result.
▪Reasoning: Martial law "can arise from necessity"; where the courts are open and functioning, the Constitution's guarantees apply: "The Constitution of the United States is a law for rulers and people, equally in war and in peace."
▪Subsequent treatment: Distinguished in *Quirin* (belligerents in wartime); reaffirmed in *Duncan*.
▪Relevance: The controlling rejection of "martial law has suspended the Constitution" theories — emergencies do not create a second legal order.

17. Ex parte Quirin, 317 U.S. 1 (1942)

▪Facts: Eight German saboteurs landed from U-boats; one claimed U.S. citizenship.
▪Issue: May unlawful combatants be tried by military commission?
▪Holding: Yes — even the citizenship claimant (he had rejoined the enemy).
▪Subsequent treatment: Widely criticized by scholars, never overruled; cited in *Hamdi*.
▪Relevance: The boundary case between *Milligan* and wartime necessity — decided on combatant status, not on claimed personal status.

18. Duncan v. Kahanamoku, 327 U.S. 304 (1946)

▪Facts: A Honolulu shipfitter was tried and convicted by a military tribunal in a boxing-incident assault after Pearl Harbor.
▪Issue: Did the Hawaiian Organon Act authorize military trial of civilians?
▪Holding: No — 6–2. Civilian criminal trials by military tribunal are unauthorized where civil courts function.
▪Relevance: Directly follows *Milligan* in peacetime emergency conditions — the "emergency powers" architecture never displaces ordinary courts.

19. Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer, 343 U.S. 579 (1952)

▪Facts: President ordered seizure of steel mills during the Korean War.
▪Issue: Does the President have inherent authority to seize private property in an emergency?
▪Holding: No — 6–3.
▪Reasoning: Justice Jackson's concurrence supplies the operative framework: (1) maximum authority with express/implied congressional consent; (2) "zone of twilight"; (3) lowest ebb against express congressional will.
▪Subsequent treatment: The master framework for every separation-of-powers dispute since.
▪Relevance: Emergencies do not create power; power flows from statute and Constitution.

20. Hamdi v. Rumsfeld, 542 U.S. 507 (2004)

▪Facts: U.S. citizen captured in Afghanistan designated an enemy combatant and detained.
▪Issue: May a citizen be indefinitely detained without process?
▪Holding: Detention authorized by the AUMF; but due process requires a meaningful opportunity to contest designation before a neutral decisionmaker.
▪Relevance: Even at the outer edge of war powers, process governs — and status is determined by *acts and records*, not by declaration.

21. Boumediene v. Bush, 553 U.S. 723 (2008)

▪Facts: Guantanamo detainees sought habeas after the DTA stripped it.
▪Issue: Does the Suspension Clause reach non-citizens at Guantanamo?
▪Holding: Yes — the writ's protection follows sovereignty's functional reach; the CSRT system was an inadequate substitute.
▪Relevance: Jurisdiction follows the Constitution's own map — not anyone's private theory of it.

E. MONETARY POWER (AGAINST FEDERAL-RESERVE AND GOLD-CLAIM ARGUMENTS)

22. Knox v. Lee, 79 U.S. (12 Wall.) 457 (1871) (Legal Tender Cases)

▪Facts: Creditor sought payment in specie; Legal Tender Acts made paper money valid tender.
▪Issue: May Congress make paper currency legal tender?
▪Holding: Yes — the monetary power is "concurrent" with war power in scope; no constitutional violation.
▪Subsequent treatment: Never overruled; the foundation of federal monetary authority.
▪Relevance: Directly answers "the Federal Reserve Act is unconstitutional" claims — Congress's monetary and banking powers were settled in 1819 and 1871.

23. Norman v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R., 294 U.S. 240 (1935)

▪Facts: Railroads' gold clauses promised payment in gold coin; Congress abrogated gold clauses in 1933.
▪Issue: Is congressional abrogation of gold clauses constitutional?
▪Holding: Yes — public policy control over monetary medium; companion cases (*Nortz*, *Perry*) reached the same result on remedies.
▪Relevance: The gold-standard claims common in "sovereign" redemption material (gold as "real money," Treasury accounts in gold) have no doctrinal support — Congress's monetary management power is plenary in its domain.

A. JUDICIAL REVIEW AND THE COURT SYSTEM

1. Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803)

▪Facts: William Marbury, one of Adams's "midnight" justices of the peace, petitioned for mandamus to deliver his commission.
▪Issue: May the Supreme Court enforce delivery by original mandamus under § 13 of the Judiciary Act?
▪Holding: Marbury had a right, but the Court cannot issue the mandamus — § 13 exceeded the Constitution's original-jurisdiction grant and is void.
▪Reasoning: "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is"; a law repugnant to the Constitution is void.
▪Subsequent treatment: Judicial review confirmed in practice by *Martin* (1816) and *Cooper v. Aaron* (1958).
▪Relevance: Jurisdiction is bounded by the Constitution itself — including this Court's.

2. Martin v. Hunter's Lessee, 14 U.S. (1 Wheat.) 304 (1816)

▪Facts: Land-confiscation dispute; Virginia courts denied federal appellate review.
▪Issue: Does the Supreme Court's appellate power extend over state court decisions?
▪Holding: Yes — § 25 of the 1789 Act valid.
▪Reasoning: Justice Story: the Constitution's judicial power is one system; uniformity of interpretation requires federal review.
▪Relevance: One integrated judiciary, appellate-supervised — not autonomous parallel systems.

3. Louisville & Nashville R.R. v. Mottley, 219 U.S. 467 (1911)

▪Facts: Railroad pass-holders sued for breach; the complaint anticipated a federal defense (the Free-Passes Act).
▪Issue: Does a *federal defense* supply federal-question jurisdiction?
▪Holding: No — the well-pleaded complaint rule; jurisdiction must appear on the face of the plaintiff's claim.
▪Subsequent treatment: Doctrine reaffirmed; and the case is the root of the rule that subject-matter jurisdiction cannot be waived or stipulated (parties' agreement cannot create jurisdiction).
▪Relevance: Consent of the parties cannot make a court competent — the true jurisdiction doctrine, as against "no contract, no jurisdiction" inversions.

B. PERSONAL JURISDICTION

4. Pennoyer v. Neff, 95 U.S. 714 (1878)

▪Facts: Neff, an Oregon nonresident served by publication, had property later attached.
▪Issue: Is a judgment valid against a nonresident served only by publication, with no in-state assets at suit?
▪Holding: No — states have plenary territorial jurisdiction; personal jurisdiction requires presence, domicile, consent, or in-state service/property.
▪Subsequent treatment: Superseded by *International Shoe*'s contacts analysis, but the consent/presence framework remains.
▪Relevance: Jurisdiction derives from territory, acts, and presence — the *opposite* of the consent-free, status-based theory.

5. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945)

▪Facts: Corporation's salesmen solicited orders in Washington; unemployment contributions assessed.
▪Issue: When does an out-of-state defendant have personal jurisdiction?
▪Holding: "Minimum contacts… such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice."
▪Relevance: The modern test — activity-based, not paper-based; a birth certificate plays no role.

6. Calder v. Jones, 465 U.S. 783 (1984)

▪Facts: Florida-based reporter sued in California over an article about a California entertainer.
▪Issue: Jurisdiction over an out-of-state writer for an injury felt in the forum?
▪Holding: Yes — the "effects test": intentional, expressly aimed at the forum with harm there.
▪Relevance: Modern personal jurisdiction is a functional analysis; jurisdictional paperwork theories have no place in it.

C. LEGISLATIVE (ARTICLE I) COURTS AND BANKRUPTCY

7. Ex parte Bakelite Corp., 279 U.S. 438 (1929)

▪Facts: Customs-court appeal questioned the tribunal's constitutional character.
▪Issue: Which courts are Article III courts, and which are legislative?
▪Holding: Congress may create legislative courts for public-rights matters (customs, patents, tax, territories, D.C.).
▪Relevance: The real "two systems" doctrine — legislative courts for defined subject matter, fully constitutional — and fully *public*.

8. Palmore v. United States, 411 U.S. 389 (1973)

▪Facts: Trial in the D.C. Superior Court (an Article I court) challenged for lack of Article III judges.
▪Issue: May Congress vest D.C.'s local courts in non-Article III judges?
▪Holding: Yes — Congress's plenary District-of-Columbia power includes a local judiciary.
▪Relevance: Territorial/local courts are constitutionally legitimate — no "second government" fiction required.

9. Northern Pipeline Construction Co. v. Marathon Pipe Line Co., 458 U.S. 50 (1982)

▪Facts: Debtor sued a non-creditor on a state-law claim in bankruptcy court.
▪Issue: May Article I bankruptcy judges adjudicate ordinary state-law suits?
▪Holding: No — 6 judges in plurality; bankruptcy courts lacked the structural safeguards of Article III for that claim.
▪Subsequent treatment: Narrowed by *Stern* and *Sharif*; bankruptcy jurisdiction refined but the system endured (28 U.S.C. § 157).
▪Relevance: The genuine constitutional limits on specialized courts — enforced by Article III itself, not by any private filing.

10. Stern v. Marshall, 564 U.S. 462 (2011)

▪Facts: Estate countersuit between the Texas oil heirs (the *Pierce*/*Anna Nicole* litigation).
▪Issue: May a bankruptcy court enter final judgment on a state-law counterclaim between non-creditors?
▪Holding: No — even with party consent by statute, Article III forbids it.
▪Relevance: Confirms that consent cannot create judicial power where the Constitution forbids — the mirror image of the "I withdraw consent" theory.

11. Wellness International Network, Ltd. v. Sharif, 575 U.S. 665 (2015)

▪Facts: Debtor's property (mere management) claim adjudicated by a bankruptcy judge.
▪Issue: May parties knowingly consent to Article I adjudication?
▪Holding: Yes — 5–4; Article III can be waived by knowing, voluntary consent.
▪Relevance: Consent is a doctrine with strict boundaries — it can *expand* forum choice for parties, but can never remove a person from jurisdiction by unilateral declaration.

12. Freytag v. Commissioner, 501 U.S. 868 (1991)

▪Facts: Tax Court special-trial judge's participation challenged.
▪Issue: Are Tax Court judges constitutionally appointed, and does the Tax Court exercise judicial power?
▪Holding: Yes appointments valid; the Tax Court exercises a "portion of the judicial power."
▪Relevance: The tax tribunal is a real court of the United States — tax jurisdiction is neither optional nor escapable by status claims.

D. CITIZENSHIP AND LEGAL PERSONHOOD

13. Dred Scott v. Sandford, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 393 (1857)

▪Facts: Enslaved man sued for freedom after residence in free territory.
▪Issue: Citizenship and the reach of the Missouri Compromise.
▪Holding: Black Americans (then) could not be citizens; the Compromise invalid. 7–2.
▪Subsequent treatment: Superseded entirely by the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments; universally condemned.
▪Relevance: Included because every citizenship history must pass through it — and because it shows the Fourteenth Amendment was the *repair*.

14. Slaughter-House Cases, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36 (1873)

▪Facts: New Orleans butchers challenged a Louisiana slaughter monopoly under the Fourteenth Amendment.
▪Issue: What rights does the Fourteenth Amendment protect?
▪Holding: The Privileges or Immunities Clause protects federal rights; state rights remain under state protection; national and state citizenship are dual.
▪Relevance: The "two citizenships" the movement half-remembers are *this* duality — both of which include the person in federal and state jurisdiction.

15. United States v. Wong Kim Ark, 169 U.S. 649 (1898)

▪Facts: Born in San Francisco to Chinese subjects (exclusion laws barred their naturalization).
▪Issue: Is he a citizen by the Fourteenth Amendment?
▪Holding: Yes — birth in the United States, of alien parents, confers citizenship (jus soli), except children of foreign armies/diplomats.
▪Relevance: The settled meaning of "subject to the jurisdiction" — complete jurisdiction, not a paperwork filter.

16. Elk v. Wilkins, 112 U.S. 94 (1884)

▪Facts: Winnebago who left his tribe sought citizenship by residence.
▪Issue: Does the Fourteenth Amendment confer citizenship by mere residence after leaving tribal life?
▪Holding: No — voluntary severance plus naturalization or congressional act required; Congress answered in 1924 (Indian Citizenship Act).
▪Relevance: Allegiance and jurisdiction are legal categories fixed by law and Congress — not by individual declaration.

17. Afroyim v. Rusk, 387 U.S. 253 (1967)

▪Facts: Polish-born naturalized citizen voted in an Israeli election; State Department revoked citizenship.
▪Issue: May citizenship be forfeited without the citizen's intent?
▪Holding: No — the Fourteenth Amendment bars involuntary citizenship loss; voting is an act of citizenship, not expatriation.
▪Relevance: citizenship may be relinquished only by *voluntary, intentional* acts (see *Terrazas*) — "self-declared expatriation" is not a legal event.

18. Vance v. Terrazas, 444 U.S. 252 (1980)

▪Facts: Dual citizen (US–Mexico) obtained Mexican citizenship and papers.
▪Issue: What must the government prove for expatriation?
▪Holding: Voluntary commission of an expatriating act plus intent to relinquish; preponderance standard suffices.
▪Relevance: The exact, judicially supervised route out of citizenship — 8 U.S.C. § 1481 procedures, not private instruments.

19. Fitisemanu v. United States (10th Cir. 2021, en banc; cert. denied Oct. 17, 2022)

▪Facts: American Samoans claimed Fourteenth Amendment birthright citizenship.
▪Issue: Does the Citizenship Clause extend to unincorporated territories?
▪Holding: No — following the Insular Cases; 8 U.S.C. § 1408's "national but not citizen" status for American Samoa upheld.
▪Relevance: The most current word on the citizenship clause's territorial reach — and the verified answer to "birth certificate nationality" claims about U.S. territories.

20. Dartmouth College v. Woodward, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 518 (1819)

▪Facts: New Hampshire tried to convert a private college to public control.
▪Issue: Is a corporate charter a contract protected by the Constitution?
▪Holding: Yes — corporations are juridical persons holding contractual rights.
▪Relevance: The legitimate root of corporate personhood — private, chartered entities; nothing here federalizes individuals into corporations.

21. Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886)

▪Facts: San Francisco's laundry ordinance administered to exclude Chinese operators; Yick Wo, an alien, prosecuted.
▪Issue: Do equal-protection guarantees protect aliens and persons (not just citizens)?
▪Holding: Yes; administration with discriminatory intent violates the Fourteenth Amendment. "Sovereignty itself is, of course, not subject to law, for it is the author and source of law" — the *government's* accountability to law.
▪Relevance: The "sovereignty" quotation so often detached from context is about governmental limits — its opposite, in application, of personal immunity.

E. MARITIME JURISDICTION

22. The Harrisburg, 119 U.S. 199 (1886)

▪Facts: Death of a passenger ashore after a maritime incident.
▪Issue: Is a tort on land maritime?
▪Holding: No — the locality test: maritime torts require injury on navigable waters.
▪Relevance: First-line authority against "admiralty on land" — the land/sea line the movement inverts.

23. Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205 (1917)

▪Facts: New York workmen's compensation award to a maritime employee.
▪Issue: May state law displace maritime remedies for maritime employment?
▪Holding: No — state laws that "work material prejudice to the characteristic features of the general maritime law" cannot stand; Congress later created exceptions (1920 amendments; LHWCA).
▪Relevance: General maritime law is federal, and its boundaries are doctrinal — not a universal commercial overlay.

24. Executive Jet Aviation, Inc. v. City of Cleveland, 418 U.S. 207 (1974)

▪Facts: Aircraft struck birds on takeoff and crashed into Lake Erie.
▪Issue: Is an aircraft crash into navigable water an admiralty tort?
▪Holding: No — locality plus "a significant relationship to traditional maritime activity."
▪Relevance: Even *on the water*, admiralty requires maritime character — decisively against "everything touching water is maritime."

25. Foremost Insurance Co. v. Richardson, 457 U.S. 668 (1982)

▪Facts: Collision of two pleasure boats.
▪Issue: Does admiralty jurisdiction cover recreational-craft collisions?
▪Holding: Yes.
▪Relevance: Shows admiralty's breadth *within* its subject matter — the accurate picture versus the straw-man version.

26. Yamaha Motor Corp. v. Calhoun, 516 U.S. 199 (1996)

▪Facts: Death of a 12-year-old on a rented waverunner in Pennsylvania waters.
▪Issue: Do state remedies for the death of a nonseafarer survive?
▪Holding: Yes — general maritime wrongful-death remedies do not displace state remedies for nonseafarers in territorial waters.
▪Relevance: Admiralty and state law interlock by subject matter — no hidden exclusivity.

27. Miles v. Apex Marine Corp., 498 U.S. 19 (1990)

▪Facts: Seaman's wrongful-death claim for loss of society damages.
▪Issue: What remedies does general maritime wrongful death provide?
▪Holding: Loss of society unavailable; uniformity rule ("*The Harrisburg* plus *Jensen*").
▪Relevance: The Court actively harmonizes maritime doctrine — evidence of a living, bounded system, not an occult jurisdiction.

3. Deep Dives on the Four Most Litigated Theories

3.1 The sovereign citizen / "individual sovereignty" cluster

Claim: a person may renounce implied federal obligations while retaining U.S. protection, often framed as "14th Amendment citizen" vs. "state citizen."

Contrary authority (verified): *Meads v. Meads*, 848 F.2d 66 (9th Cir. 1988) — the canonical American catalogue of "positivist/sovereign" arguments and their variants ("freemen," "constitutionalists," "Posse Comitatus"), all rejected; *Benabe*, 633 F.3d 581, 587 (7th Cir. 2011) — "[r]egardless of an individual's claimed status ... he is still a person subject to the revenue laws," sovereign-citizen immunity defenses "frivolous"; 8th Cir. (2024) — repeat sovereign filings forfeit appeal considerations.

Practical consequence: dismissal; sanctions; loss of appeals; state vexatious-litigant orders; in extreme cases criminal prosecution for paper terrorism.

3.2 The strawman / redemption complex

Claim: the government created a secret "strawman" (the ALL-CAPS name) holding an exemption account; A4V endorsements and UCC-1 filings can tap it.

Origins (verified): Roger Elvick, 1980s; SPLC and judicial opinions trace the lineage through Posse Comitatus circles; *United States v. Lorenzo*, 995 F.2d 1448 (5th Cir. 1993) — federal prosecutions of fictitious-obligation schemes in which Elvick was a defendant.

Contrary authority: TreasuryDirect: birth-certificate bonds "do not exist" and related schemes are fraud; the 530-judgment review (2023): universal rejection; criminal statutes: 18 U.S.C. § 514 (fictitious obligations), § 152, §§ 891–896 (false liens), 26 U.S.C. § 7206.

Practical consequence: a documented fraud economy — promoters sell templates; users absorb liability.

3.3 The admiralty/courtroom-ritual cluster

Claim: flags, the judge's presence/absence, the "star spangled banner" oath rituals, or the courtroom's geography invoke admiralty or ecclesiastical jurisdiction and void proceedings.

Contrary authority (verified): *McCann v. Greenway*, 952 F. Supp. 647 (W.D. Mo. 1997) (gold-fringe theory frivolous); broader pattern confirmed by legal scholarship (Montana Law Review 2019, Kalinowski) and fact-checks (Lead Stories 2021). Jurisdiction is determined by facts and statute, never by drapery.

Why the theory persists: it converts the observed (flags in every courtroom — required by 4 U.S.C. § 5 display conventions and VA/custom rules) into the imagined (a hidden jurisdiction).
