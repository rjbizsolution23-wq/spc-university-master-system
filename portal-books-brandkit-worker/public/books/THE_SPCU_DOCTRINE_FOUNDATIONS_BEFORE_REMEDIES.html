VOLUME I — FOUNDATIONS AND TYPES OF SOVEREIGNTY

1. Executive Overview

Sovereignty is the suprema potestas — supreme authority within a territory — and United States law distributes it across four interacting layers: the people (popular sovereignty), the national government (external and delegated sovereignty), the states (residual internal sovereignty), and the tribes (retained inherent sovereignty). Foreign states, diplomats, and territories hold distinct, statutorily and constitutionally defined immunities. Every modern U.S. doctrine of sovereignty is *institutional* — it attaches to governments, nations, and tribes — and no court has ever recognized a category of *personal* sovereignty that exempts an individual from generally applicable law. Theories of "individual sovereignty" advanced in court are rejected, and are separately classified in Volume VIII.

2. Detailed Legal Explanation — Each Doctrine

2.1 Popular sovereignty

Doctrine. The people are the ultimate source of governmental authority; governments "deriv[e] their just powers from the consent of the governed." Declaration of Independence (1776). Madison, Federalist No. 49: "the people are the only legitimate fountain of power." The Constitution was ordained by "We the People" and ratified by special conventions (Art. VII), not by the state legislatures — the structural point of *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819): the federal government "emanates from the people" and "its powers are assuredly granted" by them, not by the states.

Status: BINDING (constitutional structure).

2.2 National sovereignty (external)

Doctrine. The United States, as a nation, holds inherent external sovereignty — the power to conduct foreign affairs, wage war, admit and exclude aliens, and govern territory — not because the states delegated it but because nationhood carries it. *United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp.*, 299 U.S. 304 (1936) (Sutherland, J.): the federal government's external powers "are the resultant of ... thepower to declare and wage war ... and to effectuate it through diplomatic channels."

Status: BINDING.

2.3 State sovereignty and the Eleventh Amendment

Doctrine. States entered the Union with "residual sovereignty" over all else. But state sovereign immunity from federal suits is a constitutional rule: ratified in reaction to *Chisholm v. Georgia*, 2 U.S. (2 Dall.) 419 (1793) (suit against a state by a South Carolina creditor allowed), the Eleventh Amendment (1795) bars federal-jurisdiction suits by a citizen against his own state, and *Hans v. Louisiana*, 134 U.S. 1 (1890), extended the bar to federal-question claims. The modern line:

▪*Seminole Tribe of Fla. v. Florida*, 517 U.S. 44 (1996) — Congress may not abrogate state immunity under its Article I (Indian Commerce) powers.
▪*Alden v. Maine*, 527 U.S. 706 (1999) — immunity also bars state-court suits against states; sovereign immunity "derives from" the constitutional structure.

Exceptions (all BINDING): (1) state consent (*Lapides v. Bd. of Regents*, 535 U.S. 613 (2002) — removing from state to federal court waives immunity); (2) Congress may abrogate under § 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment (*Fitzpatrick v. Bitzer*, 427 U.S. 445 (1976); *Nev. Dep't of Human Res. v. Hibbs*, 538 U.S. 721 (2003)); (3) *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123 (1908) — prospective relief against a state officer for ongoing violations of federal law is not a suit against the state.

Status: BINDING.

2.4 Internal vs. external sovereignty

Internal sovereignty = authority over domestic affairs, distributed by federalism (see Volume II). External sovereignty = nationhood capacity, lodged exclusively in the federal government. States cannot conduct foreign policy, coin money, or enter treaties (Art. I § 10).

2.5 Territorial sovereignty

Doctrine. Congress governs territories under Art. IV § 3 cl. 2 with "plenary" power, but constitutional guarantees follow (*Downes v. Bidwell*, 182 U.S. 244 (1901), and the Insular Cases). *Puerto Rico v. Sánchez Valle*, 579 U.S. 59 (2016): Puerto Rico is not a separate sovereign for double-jeopardy purposes — sovereignty over Puerto Rico traces to Congress. *United States v. Vaello Madero*, 596 U.S. 286 (2022): Congress may exclude Puerto Rico residents from Supplemental Security Income without violating equal protection.

Status: BINDING (the Insular Cases' framework is criticized by scholars; unresolved: their future).

2.6 Indigenous and tribal sovereignty

Doctrine. Tribes retain inherent sovereignty as "distinct, independent political communities" predating the Constitution. Key authorities:

▪*Johnson v. M'Intosh*, 21 U.S. (8 Wheat.) 543 (1823) — discovery doctrine; tribes hold aboriginal title subject to the discoverer's extinguishment power.
▪*Worcester v. Georgia*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515 (1832) — tribes are "distinct political communities, having territorial boundaries, within which their authority is exclusive."
▪*United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313 (1978) — tribal sovereignty is *retained*, not *delegated*; tribal prosecution is not a second "separate sovereign" prosecution (no double jeopardy bar).
▪*Duro v. Reina*, 495 U.S. 676 (1990) — tribes lack criminal jurisdiction over nonmember Indians; *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193 (2004) — Congress may constitutionally restore it (Duro-fix legislation).
▪*Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544 (1981) — tribes generally lack civil regulatory authority over nonmembers on non-Indian fee land, subject to the "Montana exceptions."
▪*McGirt v. Oklahoma*, 590 U.S. 250 (2020) — the Muscogee (Creek) Reservation was never disestablished; state criminal courts lacked jurisdiction over crimes by Indians on it.
▪*Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta*, 597 U.S. 629 (2022) — states have concurrent jurisdiction over crimes by non-Indians against Indians on Indian land absent federal preemption.
▪*Haaland v. Brackeen*, 599 U.S. 255 (2023) — the Indian Child Welfare Act is constitutional; Congress's Indian-affairs power is broad.
▪Tribal sovereign immunity from suit: *Kiowa Tribe v. Mfg. Tech., Inc.*, 523 U.S. 751 (1998).

Status: BINDING, and currently evolving (state/tribal jurisdiction litigation continues into the 2025–26 term).

2.7 Diplomatic immunity

Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (1961), arts. 22–31: full immunity for diplomatic agents from criminal jurisdiction, qualified immunity civilly; inviolability of premises. Implemented in U.S. law (22 U.S.C. §§ 254a–254d). Status: BINDING (ratified treaty + statute).

2.8 Foreign sovereign immunity

Doctrine. From absolute immunity (*The Schooner Exchange v. McFaddon*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 116 (1812)) to the restrictive theory (U.S. Dept. of State "Tate Letter," 1952), codified in the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act of 1976, 28 U.S.C. §§ 1602–1611 — the exclusive basis for suit (Argentina v. Amerada Hess, 488 U.S. 428 (1989)). Core exceptions: waiver; commercial activity (*Republic of Argentina v. Weltover*, 504 U.S. 607 (1992)); expropriation "in violation of international law" — *German v. Philipp*, 592 U.S. 118 (2021) (genocide exception reaches only genocide, and requires taking property in violation of the law of nations); *Hungary v. Simon*, 590 U.S. 30 (2020). *Republic of Austria v. Altmann*, 541 U.S. 677 (2004) — FSIA applies retroactively.

Status: BINDING.

2.9 Sovereign immunity of the United States

The United States is immune from suit without express statutory consent, strictly construed (*Lane v. Peña*, 518 U.S. 187 (1996)); principal waivers: Federal Tort Claims Act (28 U.S.C. § 1346(b)); Tucker Act (28 U.S.C. § 1491); Little Tucker Act; Bankruptcy Code § 106. Status: BINDING.

2.10 Sovereign equality of states

UN Charter art. 2(1) ("Sovereign equality of all its Members"); *Island of Palmas* (Neth. v. U.S.), 2 R.I.I.A. 829 (Perm. Ct. Arb. 1928) (Huber, arb.): sovereignty is "the right to exercise ... the functions of a State to the exclusion of any other State." Equal states, unequal power — equality is a *legal* rule.

2.11 Self-determination

UN Charter art. 1(2); ICCPR art. 1 ("All peoples have the right of self-determination"). Legally operative forms: decolonization, internal self-government. Secession has no general right in international law: *Reference re Secession of Quebec*, [1998] 2 S.C.R. 217 (Can.); *Accordance with International Law of the Unilateral Declaration of Independence of Kosovo*, Advisory Opinion, 2010 I.C.J. 141 (declaration did not violate international law, but the Court declined to say international law confers a right to secede). Domestically: *Texas v. White*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 700 (1869) — the Union is "indissoluble"; there is no state right of unilateral secession.

Status: BINDING/BEST-READ — and the decisive answer to "sovereign exits" theories (Volume VIII).

2.12 De facto vs. de jure sovereignty

A recognition-law distinction (Lauterpacht, *Recognition in International Law* (1947)): de jure recognition is the legal acknowledgment of statehood/government; de facto recognition is provisional acknowledgment of effective control. Status: PERSUASIVE (customary practice).

2.13 Parliamentary / constitutional sovereignty (comparative)

United Kingdom: Parliament is the supreme lawmaking body (A.V. Dicey, *Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution* (1885)); in the U.S., "constitutional sovereignty" — the Constitution itself is supreme (Art. VI; *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1 (1958): "the federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution"). Detailed treatment: Volume XI.

2.14 Federalism and reserved powers

Tenth Amendment: powers not delegated nor prohibited are "reserved to the States respectively, or to the people." Anti-commandeering: *Murphy v. National Collegiate Athletic Ass'n*, 584 U.S. 363 (2018) — Congress may not compel state officers to execute federal law, but states may not nullify federal law either.

2.15 Citizenship, nationality, domicile, residency

Status concepts (full treatment: Volume V): citizenship (8 U.S.C. § 1401 — birth in the U.S.; § 1431 et seq. — derivation; § 1421 — naturalization), nationality (includes noncitizen nationals of American Samoa), domicile (the one place of true, fixed, permanent home — Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws §§ 11–23), residence (mere physical presence, legally weaker than domicile). None of these statuses exempts a person from generally applicable law; claiming otherwise was rejected in *Meads v. Meads*, 848 F.2d 66 (9th Cir. 1988), and *United States v. Benabe*, 633 F.3d 581 (7th Cir. 2011).

2.16 Historical "individual sovereignty" theories — judicial treatment

Founding-era usage: Wilson and Iredell, concurring in *Chisholm*, described "sovereignty of the people" in rhetorically individualist terms; Blackstone described "absolute rights" of persons. Modern courts read none of this as personal exemption from law. Controlling rejection: *Meads v. Meads*, 848 F.2d 66, 68–69 (9th Cir. 1988) (cataloguing "individual sovereignty," "freemen," "Posse Comitatus," and related arguments as without foundation); *United States v. Benabe*, 633 F.3d 581, 587 (7th Cir. 2011) ("Regardless of an individual's claimed status of descent, be it as a 'sovereign citizen,' a 'secured-party creditor,' or a 'flesh-and-blood human being' ... that person is not beyond the jurisdiction of the courts."); the Seventh Circuit has called "sovereign-citizen immunity" defenses "frivolous" (see *United States v. Willis*, 8th Cir. 2024, collecting). Status: REJECTED. Full dossier: Volume VIII.

3. Historical Foundations

Sovereignty theory: Jean Bodin, *Six Books of the Commonwealth* (1576) (indivisible supreme power); Hobbes, *Leviathan* (1651); Emer de Vattel, *The Law of Nations* (1758) (states as moral persons). The American innovation: sovereignty split between a national government of enumerated powers and states of reserved powers, grounded in the people — the architecture *McCulloch* and *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1 (1824), map.

4. Practical Legal Applications

▪Choosing the defendant controls Eleventh Amendment exposure (state vs. officer; prospective vs. retrospective relief).
▪Tribal jurisdiction analysis runs through the Montana framework (civil) and McGirt/Castro-Huerta (criminal).
▪FSIA is a mandatory screen — service under § 1608 and exhaustion; a missed FSIA argument forfeits nothing else but dooms the case.
▪Status questions (citizenship, domicile, nationality) are governed by statute and treaty — not by declaration or paperwork choices.

5. Unresolved Questions

▪The future of the Insular Cases (called into question but not overruled; *Vaello Madero* declined to revisit them).
▪The outer bounds of tribal civil jurisdiction over nonmembers after *Castro-Huerta*.
▪Scope of § 5 abrogation after *Collins v. Yellen*, 594 U.S. 220 (2021), narrowing *Fitzpatrick* for claims against agencies' officers.

VOLUME II — CONSTITUTIONAL AUTHORITY AND GOVERNMENT STRUCTURE

1. Executive Overview

Federal authority rests on enumeration (Art. I § 8), supremacy (Art. VI), and separation of powers (the tripartite structure). Judicial review (*Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803)) makes the courts the expositors of that authority — a role reinforced in *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo* (U.S. June 28, 2024), which overruled *Chevron* deference and directed that courts, not agencies, decide questions of law. As of November 18, 2026, the governing separation-of-powers landscape includes *SEC v. Jarkesy* (June 27, 2024) and *FCC v. Consumers' Research* (June 27, 2025).

2. Enumerated Powers

▪Necessary and Proper Clause, Art. I § 8 cl. 18: *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819) — Congress may choose any rationally related means to execute enumerated powers; states may not impede federal instrumentalities.
▪Commerce Clause, Art. I § 8 cl. 3: *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1 (1824); outer expansion *Wickard v. Filburn*, 317 U.S. 111 (1942); modern limits *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549 (1995) (noneconomic gun possession), *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598 (2000) (VAWA civil remedy), *NFIB v. Sebelius*, 567 U.S. 519 (2012) (Medicaid coercion).
▪Taxing/Spending: Art. I § 8 cl. 1; *Biden v. Nebraska*, 600 U.S. 477 (2023) (major-questions approach to spending power).

3. Supremacy and Judicial Review

Art. VI cl. 2. *Martin v. Hunter's Lessee*, 14 U.S. (1 Wheat.) 304 (1816) (federal appellate power over state courts on federal questions). *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1 (1958): "the federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution" — every state and federal officer is bound. There is no procedure by which a private "declaration" or filing removes a person from this structure.

5. Incorporation of the Bill of Rights

*Barron v. Baltimore*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 243 (1833) (no incorporation against states pre-1868) → through the Fourteenth Amendment: *Gitlow v. New York*, 268 U.S. 652 (1925); *Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145 (1968); *McDonald v. Chicago*, 561 U.S. 742 (2010); *Timbs v. Indiana*, 586 U.S. 146 (2019) (excessive fines). Nearly the entire Bill of Rights now binds the states — relevant because "state citizen" theories assume the opposite.

6. Territorial Government and the District of Columbia

▪Territories: *American Insurance Co. v. Canter*, 26 U.S. (1 Pet.) 511 (1828) — Congress may create territorial (Article IV) courts; *Downes v. Bidwell*, 182 U.S. 244 (1901) (Insular Cases).
▪D.C.: Article I § 8 cl. 17 gives Congress "exclusive Legislation" over the seat of government. The Organic Act of 1871 created a territorial-style government for the District; it was replaced in 1874 and superseded by a commissioner government (1878); the Home Rule Act of 1973, Pub. L. 93-198, created the current elected government. *Palmore v. United States*, 411 U.S. 389 (1973) — D.C. local courts are valid Article I courts. Nothing in any of these acts converted the United States into a corporation (Volume VII dissects the 28 U.S.C. § 3002(15) definition that fuels this claim).

7. Emergency Powers and Martial Law

▪Statutory architecture: Trading with the Enemy Act (1917); National Emergencies Act (1976) — terminated all then-existing emergencies and created the declaration/termination regime; IEEPA (1977).
▪Constitutional limits: *Ex parte Milligan*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2 (1866) — no military trial of civilians where civil courts are open; *Duncan v. Kahanamoku*, 327 U.S. 304 (1946); *Youngstown* (1952). *Trump v. Hawaii*, 138 S. Ct. 2392 (2018) formally repudiated *Korematsu*.
▪Conclusion (BINDING): No declared emergency suspends the Constitution or the civilian courts. "The emergency is still on" is not a legal argument; it has been rejected wherever asserted (Volume VIII).

8. Practical Applications

▪A party challenging federal action must attack the *specific* statute's delegation or application — *Loper Bright* makes statutory-text arguments more powerful in court than they have been since 1984; *Jarkesy* restores jury rights in common-law-like penalty actions.
▪None of this creates a "private law" channel, a "de facto" court, or a consent-based exemption. The lawful paths are the ordinary ones: motions, appeals, constitutional litigation.

VOLUME V — LEGAL PERSONHOOD, CITIZENSHIP, AND POLITICAL STATUS

1. Executive Overview

American law recognizes persons — natural and artificial — and attaches rights and duties to status (citizenship, nationality, domicile). It recognizes no third category of person (no "strawman," no "flesh-and-blood human" distinct from the "legal person"), and no political status a person can declare that exempts them from law. Every component of this volume is BINDING as of November 18, 2026, including *Trump v. Barbara* (U.S. June 30, 2026), which reaffirmed birthright citizenship.

2. Natural and Legal Persons

▪1 U.S.C. § 1: in federal statutes, "person" and "whoever" "include corporations, companies, associations, firms, partnerships, societies, and joint stock companies, as well as individuals." The statutory word *includes* ordinary human beings; it does not exclude them.
▪Corporate personhood: *Trustees of Dartmouth College v. Woodward*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 518 (1819) (charter as contract); *Pembina Consol. Silver Mining Co. v. Pennsylvania*, 125 U.S. 181 (1888); *Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific R.R.*, 118 U.S. 394 (1886) (equal-protection application via headnote — historically contested); *First National Bank v. Bellotti*, 435 U.S. 765 (1978); *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010); *Burwell v. Hobby Lobby Stores*, 573 U.S. 682 (2014).
▪Key point: treating a corporation as a "person" *adds* obligations and protections; it never subtracts them from humans. The human being in the courtroom IS the "person" the statute names. *United States v. Benabe*, 633 F.3d 581, 587 (7th Cir. 2011): whatever one's claimed status — "'sovereign citizen,' 'secured-party creditor,' or 'flesh-and-blood human being'" — one remains a person subject to law.

3. Birth Registration — What the Documents Are

▪Birth certificates are state vital-records documents (state vital statistics acts; model Uniform Vital Records Act). They establish identity and citizenship facts.
▪There is no federal "birth bond," no Treasury account, no CUSIP-numbered security tied to a birth certificate. TreasuryDirect's official fraud advisory states plainly: [Birth Certificate Bonds — "This redemption scheme is a fraud"](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/laws-and-regulations/fraud/birth-certificate-bonds/). The Social Security Administration similarly warns against "birth certificate bond" and related claims.
▪Status: REJECTED/UNSUPPORTED — and actively prosecuted when used (18 U.S.C. §§ 891–896, false liens; 26 U.S.C. § 6702, frivolous submissions).

4. Citizenship — Current, Controlling Law

▪Birthright citizenship (jus soli): *United States v. Wong Kim Ark*, 169 U.S. 649 (1898) — the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause makes citizens of all persons born in the United States (subject to limited exceptions: children of foreign diplomats, hostile occupiers, and historically, tribes).
▪2025–2026 litigation (verified this session): Executive Order 14160 (Jan. 20, 2025) directed agencies to deny citizenship to certain U.S.-born children. *Trump v. CASA, Inc.*, 604 U.S. ___ (June 27, 2025), first curtailed the universal injunctions issued against the Order — without reaching the merits. Then, on **June 30, 2026, in *Trump v. Barbara*, No. 25-365, the Supreme Court struck the Order down as facially unconstitutional**, holding the Citizenship Clause means what *Wong Kim Ark* said it means. ([Opinion PDF](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25-365_4hdj.pdf); [SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/06/supreme-court-strikes-down-trumps-order-ending-birthright-citizenship/).)
▪Statutory citizenship: 8 U.S.C. § 1401 (birth and derivation); § 1421 (naturalization); Indian Citizenship Act of 1924.
▪Slaughter-House Cases, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36 (1873): dual character of state and national citizenship.

|---|---|---|

| Citizenship | Nationality under 8 U.S.C. § 1401 or naturalization | Jurisdiction, voting, consular protection |

| Domicile | One's single true, fixed, permanent home (Restatement (Second) Conflicts §§ 11–23) | Diversity jurisdiction; probate; taxation |

| Residence | Physical presence, even extended | Some statutory benefits; tax contact |

A. MAXIMS OF EQUITY (with their real, citable operation)

1. "Equity will not suffer a wrong to be without a remedy." Origin of equitable relief where law's remedies were inadequate; now embodied in remedies discretion (e.g., specific performance, injunctions — Fed. R. Civ. P. 65; 28 U.S.C. § 2201 (declaratory relief)).

2. "Equity follows the law." Equity supplements, never abolishes, legal rights — the reason the 1938 merger of law and equity (Fed. R. Civ. P. 2) preserved *substantive* distinctions.

3. "He who seeks equity must do equity." Relief conditioned on the plaintiff's own conduct — regularly invoked against litigants advancing pseudo-instruments.

4. "He who comes into equity must come with clean hands." Conduct-based bar; the doctrine courts deploy against parties whose filings were themselves fraudulent.

5. "Equity delights to do justice, not by halves." Full relief framework (Rule 65(d) scope; supplemental decrees).

6. "Equity looks to intent rather than form." True — and the reason *formal labels* ("I declared myself a trust") do not control substance.

7. "Equity regards as done that which ought to be done." Constructive trusts and equitable liens rest here (Restatement (Third) of Restitution).

8. "Equity imputes an intention to fulfill an obligation." Performance of duties presumed in fiduciary relationships.

9. "Equity acts in personam." Personal orders against parties — and the basis of contempt powers against litigants regardless of claimed status.

10. "Equity aids the vigilant, not those who slumber." Laches (Petrella v. Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, 572 U.S. 663 (2014) — its scope against statutes of limitation).

11. "Where equities are equal, the law prevails." Legal title and priority rules decide stale contests.

12. "Between equal equities, priority in time prevails." BFP v. Resolution Trust Corp., 511 U.S. 531 (1994) (context: foreclosures).

13. "Equality is equity." Marshaling and pro-rata doctrines in creditor law.

14. "Once a trustee, always a trustee." Trustee accountability follows the office.

B. TRUST LAW — THE REAL ARCHITECTURE

Elements (Restatement (Third) of Trusts; Uniform Trust Code § 402): (1) *settlor* with capacity and intent; (2) ascertainable *beneficiary* (with charitable-trust exceptions); (3) identified *trust property* (corpus); (4) *trustee* with duties; (5) valid *purpose*.

Types: express, resulting, constructive (imposed by law against fraud/breach — e.g., fraudulent conveyances, 11 U.S.C. § 548), testamentary, inter vivos, charitable, and business/escrow variants.

Fiduciary duties: loyalty (no self-dealing), prudence (UTC § 804), impartiality (§ 803), duty to account (§ 813). Breach remedies: surcharge, disgorgement, removal (§ 706), constructive trust.

Persona-relevant caution (verified): trusts marketed as "pure," "constitutional," "sovereign," "ecclesiastical," or "unincorporated business organization" packages for hiding income/assets are the IRS-documented Abusive Trust Tax Evasion Schemes (IRS pages current 2026; DOJ injunctions 2002). Red flags per IRS: grantor retains control; no independent trustee; "trust" files nothing. Legitimate church autonomy (*Watson v. Jones*, 80 U.S. 679 (1871); *Hosanna-Tabor*, 565 U.S. 171 (2012); *Our Lady of Guadalupe*, 591 U.S. 732 (2020)) concerns internal church governance — it does not shield secular assets or income.

C. COMMERCIAL LAW — UCC ARTICLES 1, 3, 9 (OPERATIVE REAL CONTENT)

Article 1: "Good faith" = honesty in fact (1-201(b)(20)); "person" = individual or organization (1-201(b)(30)); freedom-of-contract (1-302).

Article 3 — Negotiable Instruments: elements (3-104: unconditional promise/order to pay a fixed sum, to order or bearer, on demand or definite time, signed); negotiation (3-201); holder in due course (3-302: value, good faith, no notice of claims/defects); liability requires signature (3-401); presentment, notice of dishonor (3-503), protest (3-501). The A4V/"signature is an endorsement" inversion has no support in any of these sections — liability attaches by *signature on an instrument*, not by an instrument's existence.

Article 9 — Secured Transactions: attachment (9-203: value given; debtor rights in collateral; authenticated agreement); perfection (filing, possession, control — 9-301 et seq.); priority (9-317 et seq.); debtor-name rules (9-503); filing-office integrity — states' responses to fraudulent filings (NASS 2014). "Secured party" is a role in a collateral transaction — not a personal status, and no Article 9 mechanism separates a natural person from liability.

Law Merchant (the historical base): medieval merchant custom absorbed into the common law of contracts and negotiable instruments — *Clearfield Trust Co. v. United States*, 318 U.S. 363 (1943) (federal commercial paper law); the Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws and UCC in origin reference the lex mercatoria's absorption. The legitimate "Law Merchant" is this absorbed body of commercial doctrine — not a parallel private jurisdiction.

D. GOVERNMENT ENTITIES — THE REAL TAXONOMY

▪Municipal corporations: state-chartered local governments (counties, cities) — the actual meaning of "body corporate" in American law.
▪Government corporations: Congressionally chartered entities (e.g., USPS, Amtrak) — statutory creatures with defined powers.
▪The United States: a constitutional republic acting through enumerated powers — with definitions of "United States" in each title scoped to that title's context (26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1) (internal revenue); 28 U.S.C. § 3002(15) (debt-collection procedure — verified this session)).
▪The District of Columbia: a federal district whose 1871 Act constituted a municipal government ("body corporate for municipal purposes") — verified against the Act's text and PolitiFact's 2024 fact-check.

2.4 The Act of 1871 — the real story

Act of February 21, 1871, ch. 62, 16 Stat. 419 (An Act to provide a Government for the District of Columbia). It consolidated the city of Washington, Georgetown, and Washington County into a single municipal government for the District, exercised under Congress's Article I § 8 cl. 17 power. It was a local reorganization. Congress replaced that form of government in 1874 and established a three-commissioner government in 1878; full local home rule came only with the District of Columbia Self-Government and Governmental Reorganization Act of 1973, Pub. L. 93-198 (D.C. Code § 1-201 et seq.).

What it did NOT do: create "a second Constitution," incorporate the United States as a private corporation, or suspend anything. *Palmore v. United States*, 411 U.S. 389 (1973), confirms D.C. local courts are valid Article I courts; *District of Columbia v. Heller*, 554 U.S. 570 (2008), applies the Constitution to D.C. without hesitation. 1. Declaration of Independence (1776) — the colonial grievance record against admiralty & courts

▪[verbatim] "He has obstructed the Administration of Justice, by refusing his Assent to Laws for establishing Judiciary powers."
▪[verbatim] "For depriving us in many cases, of the benefits of Trial by Jury."
▪[verbatim] "For transporting us beyond Seas to be tried for pretended offences."

The American response was to constitutionalize jury trial and civilian control of courts — not to abolish admiralty, which art. III then wrote directly into the new frame of government.

2. Articles of Confederation (1781) — the defect that forced the 1787 fix

▪Art. IX gave Congress authority to determine "all cases of captures," "piracies and felonies committed on the high seas" (paraphrase of the grant), but created no general federal judiciary — disputes were resolved by state courts and ad hoc tribunals, and the Union could not compel compliance.

3. Constitution of the United States (1787) — the operative text

▪Art. III, § 1: "The judicial Power of the United States, shall be vested in one supreme Court, and in such inferior Courts as the Congress may from time to time ordain and establish." [verbatim]
▪Art. III, § 2, cl. 1: the judicial power extends "to all Cases of admiralty and maritime Jurisdiction" [verbatim] — a *subject-matter* grant.
▪Art. I, § 8, cl. 14: Congress's power over "militia" and courts for the government of the armed forces (modern UCMJ rests on it).

4. Judiciary Act of 1789, § 9 (1 Stat. 76–77) — the saving-to-suitors clause

▪[verbatim] "…saving to suitors, in all cases, the right of a common law remedy, where the common law is competent to give it."
▪What it means (settled): federal district courts get exclusive admiralty cognizance over in rem matters (arrest of vessels, maritime liens), while state courts retain concurrent jurisdiction over in personam maritime claims that the common law can remedy. Modern codification: 28 U.S.C. § 1333.

5. The Federalist — the design logic

▪No. 78 (Hamilton) [verbatim]: "The judiciary, on the contrary, has neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment" — courts decide; they cannot enforce or appropriate. Judicial review follows from constitutional supremacy.
▪No. 80 (Hamilton): admiralty jurisdiction belongs to the federal courts because maritime disputes are inter-jurisdictional by nature (paraphrase).
▪No. 81: state courts and federal courts coexist; federal review protects uniformity.

6. Blackstone, Commentaries (1765–69), Book III — the English baseline

▪Courts of Admiralty (ch. 5): jurisdiction over maritime contracts and torts "arising upon the high seas," with jury trial replaced by civilian witnesses (paraphrase of Book III's description). Key point for the dossier: the common-law courts and Admiralty coexisted with a boundary line — the shore.

7. Story, Commentaries on the Constitution (1833), Book III — the American synthesis

▪The admiralty grant was adopted to secure uniformity in maritime affairs; "the boundaries of admiralty jurisdiction are not easy to define" (paraphrase of Story's analysis, § 1660s) — the line the Supreme Court has drawn case-by-case ever since (*Harrisburg*, *Executive Jet*).

8. The Reconstruction Amendments — the second founding

▪13th (1865): abolished slavery — superseding *Dred Scott*'s regime.
▪14th (1868): birthright citizenship, due process, equal protection; dual citizenship (*Slaughter-House*).
▪15th (1870): race nondiscrimination in voting.
▪Together with the 1868 Expatriation Act's declaration of the right of expatriation, these — not the Act of 1871 — are the documents that define modern citizenship's legal architecture.

VOLUME III — COMPLETE ADMIRALTY AND MARITIME LAW

1. Executive Overview

Admiralty law is a specialized federal subject-matter jurisdiction governing maritime commerce, torts on navigable waters, seamen, vessels, and salvage. It exists alongside — not instead of — ordinary civil jurisdiction. The claims that admiralty law secretly governs every citizen, courtroom, or birth certificate are pseudolegal inventions rejected by controlling courts (Section 10 below and Volume VIII).

2. Historical Foundations

| Era | Source | Contribution |

|---|---|---|

| Antiquity | Rhodian Sea Law (customs of Rhodes, compiled in the Byzantine *Nomos Rhodion Nautikos*) | Earliest systematic maritime customs (jettison, collision) |

| ~1150–1190 | Rolls of Oléron (Atlantic/La Rochelle; English usage attributed to Eleanor of Aquitaine's era) | Judgment-based code for masters, crews, merchants |

| ~13th–15th c. | Laws of Wisby; Hanseatic codes | Baltic commercial law |

| ~14th c. | Consolato del Mare (Barcelona) | Comprehensive Mediterranean code |

| 14th–19th c. | English High Court of Admiralty; *Black Book of the Admiralty* | Instance and prize jurisdiction; constant friction with common-law courts |

| 1758–1805 | Vattel; Blackstone | The treatise vocabulary the Framers absorbed |

Colonial America used vice-admiralty courts (notably for Navigation Acts enforcement — a grievance in the Declaration of Independence). After 1776, the Articles of Confederation left admiralty largely to the states — one of the failures that motivated the Constitution.

3. Constitutional and Statutory Basis

▪Article III, § 2: judicial power extends to "all Cases of admiralty and maritime Jurisdiction."
▪Judiciary Act of 1789, § 9: district-court cognizance, *exclusive* over civil admiralty causes — "saving to suitors, in all cases, the right of a common law remedy where the common law is competent to give it." Codified today at 28 U.S.C. § 1333.
▪*De Lovio v. Boit*, 7 F. Cas. 418 (C.C.D. Mass. 1815) (Story, J.) — the founding-era defense of full federal admiralty jurisdiction; *The Genesee Chief*, 53 U.S. (12 How.) 443 (1851) — admiralty extends to the Great Lakes and all navigable waters, overruling the tidal-waters limit of *The Thomas Jefferson*, 10 U.S. (10 Wheat.) 428 (1825).
▪Procedure: Supplemental Rules for Certain Admiralty and Maritime Claims (Rules B–G) — attachment (B), suits in rem (C, D), vessel arrest/forfeiture (G).

4. Jurisdiction Tests (BINDING)

1. Tort (maritime but non-vessel): *Executive Jet Aviation v. City of Cleveland*, 418 U.S. 669 (1974) — locality plus "a significant relationship to traditional maritime activity"; refined in *Sisson v. Diamond*, 496 U.S. 708 (1990), and *Jerome B. Grubart, Inc. v. Great Lakes Dredge & Dock Co.*, 513 U.S. 527 (1995) (location + connection test). *Foremost Insurance Co. v. Richardson*, 457 U.S. 668 (1982) — collisions between commercial vessels are covered even off navigable waters.

2. "Vessel": *Lozman v. City of Riviera Beach*, 568 U.S. 115 (2013) — "a reasonable observer, looking to the home's physical characteristics and activities" test; *Lozman v. City of Riviera Beach (II)*, 585 U.S. 87 (2018) — maritime lien requires "some realistic connection to ... maritime activity."

3. Contract: *Kossick v. United Fruit*, 365 U.S. 731 (1961); *Norfolk Southern R.R. v. James N. Kirby, Sons*, 543 U.S. 14 (2004) — bills of lading' incorporation of arbitration clauses governs intermodal carriers.

4. Navigable waters: *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557 (1871) ("navigable waters of the United States"); *The Robert W. Parsons*, 191 U.S. 17 (1903) (capable of use for interstate/foreign commerce).

5. Core Doctrines and Remedies

▪General maritime law is "a body of law" courts apply as part of the federal judicial power (*Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen*, 244 U.S. 205 (1917)); it yields to contrary federal statute (*Miles v. Apex Marine Corp.*, 498 U.S. 19 (1990)) and yields to state law in gaps absent conflict (*Yamaha Motor Corp. v. Calhoun*, 516 U.S. 199 (1996)).
▪Maritime liens: in rem enforceable liens arising by operation of law (*The Blaze Star*, 127 U.S. 84 (1888)); exclusive in rem jurisdiction (*The Plymouth*, 70 U.S. (3 Wall.) 20 (1865)).
▪Salvage & general average: *Columbus-America Discovery Group v. Atlantic Mutual Ins. Co.*, 974 F.2d 450 (4th Cir. 1992); *Treasure Salvors, Inc. v. Unidentified Wrecked Vessel*, 458 U.S. 670 (1982); York-Antwerp Rules; Abandoned Shipwreck Act, 43 U.S.C. §§ 2101–2106.
▪Carriage of goods: Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 46 U.S.C. §§ 30701–30707; Harter Act; bills of lading doctrine (*Kirby*, above).
▪Marine insurance: state law governs absent conflict (*Wilburn Boat Co. v. Fireman's Fund Ins. Co.*, 348 U.S. 310 (1955)).
▪Limitation of Liability Act, 46 U.S.C. §§ 30501–30511: shipowner may cap liability at vessel value; *Lewis v. Lewis & Clark Marine, Inc.*, 519 U.S. 215 (1997). Recent: *Great Lakes Insurance SE v. Raiders Retreat Realty Co.*, 601 U.S. 659 (U.S. Feb. 21, 2024) — choice-of-law clauses in maritime contracts are presumptively enforceable absent conflict with federal statute.

6. Seamen and Maritime Workers

▪Jones Act (Merchant Marine Act 1920, § 33; now 46 U.S.C. § 30104): negligence remedy; seaman status under *Chandris, Inc. v. Latsis*, 515 U.S. 347 (1990) (substantial connection — the "30%" rule).
▪Maintenance and cure; unseaworthiness; punitive damages available (*Atlantic Sounding Co. v. Townsend*, 557 U.S. 404 (2009)).
▪LHWCA, 33 U.S.C. § 901 et seq. — administrative compensation for harbor workers; *Southwest Marine, Inc. v. Gizoni*, 502 U.S. 81 (1991) (remedies are mutually exclusive).

7. Flag State, Port State, and the Law of the Sea

▪Contacts analysis: *Lauritzen v. Larsen*, 345 U.S. 571 (1953); *Hellenic Lines Ltd. v. Rhoditis*, 398 U.S. 306 (1970).
▪Prize law: *The Prize Cases*, 67 U.S. (2 Black) 635 (1863); *Brown v. United States*, 12 U.S. (8 Cranch) 110 (1814).
▪UNCLOS (opened 1982; in force 1994): the United States is not a party — the Senate has never given consent; U.S. practice largely tracks it unilaterally (CRS, IF12578: "The United States is not a party to UNCLOS, but related U.S. law largely comports with its provisions"). VOLUME IV — SEPARATE COURTS AND JUDICIAL JURISDICTION

1. Executive Overview

The United States has many courts, but they all trace to identifiable constitutional or statutory authority, publish their decisions, and apply the Constitution. The architecture:

1. Article III courts (life-tenured judges): 94 district courts; 13 courts of appeals (12 regional + Federal Circuit); the Supreme Court.

2. Article I legislative courts (Congress creates under enumerated powers).

3. Article IV territorial courts.

4. State courts (sovereigns under their own constitutions).

5. Tribal courts (inherent sovereignty).

6. Administrative adjudicators (executive power).

The theory that any of these is a "secret" or "parallel" system without constitutional constraint has no support in any authority — and the doctrine of *strictly limited* subject-matter jurisdiction is the opposite of secret: it is enforced by courts on their own motion.

2. Article III Courts

Created under Art. III (life tenure, salary protection — *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803)); *Martin v. Hunter's Lessee*, 14 U.S. (1 Wheat.) 304 (1816) (federal appellate power over state courts). Jurisdiction is statutory today: federal question (28 U.S.C. § 1331), diversity (§ 1332, > $75,000, *Strawbridge v. Curtiss*, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 267 (1806) complete diversity), admiralty (§ 1333), plus specialty grants.

▪*Ex parte Bakelite Corp.*, 279 U.S. 438 (1929): categories of public-right disputes may go to legislative courts.
▪*Palmore v. United States*, 411 U.S. 389 (1973): D.C. local courts are constitutional.
▪Bankruptcy courts: *Northern Pipeline Constr. Co. v. Marathon Pipe Line Co.*, 458 U.S. 50 (1982) (Art. I bankruptcy judges cannot exercise ordinary Art. III jurisdiction over common-law suits); *Stern v. Marshall*, 564 U.S. 462 (2011); *Wellness Int'l Network v. Sharif*, 575 U.S. 665 (2015) (party consent permits).

▪Military: *Ex parte Milligan*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2 (1866); *Solorio v. United States*, 483 U.S. 435 (1987); U.S. Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces.

Administrative adjudication of genuinely "public rights" (taxes, benefits, immigration) continues.

▪Tribal courts: *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313 (1978) (retained sovereignty); *Duro*, *Lara*, *McGirt* (2020), *Oklahoma v. Castro-Huerta* (2022), *Haaland v. Brackeen* (2023) — see Volume I § 2.6.

6. The Merger of Law and Equity

▪FRCP Rule 2 (1938): "There is one form of action — the civil action."
▪*Beacon Theatres, Inc. v. Westover*, 359 U.S. 500 (1959); *Dairy Queen, Inc. v. Wood*, 369 U.S. 469 (1962): legal issues get jury trial first.

Knowledge-base companion — the persona's namesake domain, accurately grounded

1. The canon-law tradition (historical foundation)

▪Corpus Juris Canonici (Gratian's Decretum and successors) — the Church's private-law system for clergy discipline, marriage, and benefices (12th–16th c.) [K].
▪England: ecclesiastical courts (consistory, archdeaconry) held jurisdiction over marriage, wills, and probate until the mid-19th century (parish "courts Christian"); the 1857 Matrimonial Causes Act transferred marriage/divorce to civil courts [K].
▪America: no national establishment (First Amendment, 1791); state establishments ended by 1833 (Massachusetts last). Ecclesiastical courts as organs of state power do not exist in the United States — what exists is *church autonomy*, a set of constitutional doctrines protecting voluntary religious associations [K].

3. The trust interface — legitimate and flagged

▪Legitimate: church/charitable trusts are real (UTC charitable-trust provisions; Restatement (Third) of Trusts ch. 26); *Jones v. Wolf*'s neutral-principles route expressly contemplates church trusts recorded in corporate charters and deeds [K].
▪Flagged [V]: "ecclesiastical trust," "pure trust," "sovereign trust," "constitutional trust" packages marketed for tax evasion are the IRS-documented Abusive Trust Tax Evasion Schemes; DOJ enjoined promoters (2002). ▪Religious arbitration: beth din and church tribunals operate as *contractual* arbitration under state arbitration acts — their awards are enforceable only through the same civil machinery as any other arbitration, within constitutional limits (paraphrase of settled arbitration law [K]).

▪*Employment Division v. Smith*, 494 U.S. 872 (1990): neutral, generally applicable laws prevail over free-exercise challenges [K].
▪*United States v. Lee*, 455 U.S. 252 (1982) [V]: Amish employer's religious objection did not exempt him from Social Security taxes.

1. Foundational Definition and Constitutional Anchor:

An Express Trust under the Common Law (historically termed a Common-Law Pure Trust or Massachusetts Trust) is a private contractual fiduciary relationship created by a Declaration or Indenture of Trust whereby a Settlor/Grantor conveys legal title in property (the Trust Res or Corpus) to one or more Trustees to hold, manage, and administer for the equitable benefit of designated Beneficiaries (or Certificate Holders). Its contractual obligation is protected under Article I, Section 10, Clause 1 of the United States Constitution ('No State shall... pass any... Law impairing the Obligation of Contracts') and traditional Chancery Equity jurisprudence.

2. Alfred D. Chandler — Express Trusts under the Common Law (1912):

Alfred Dupont Chandler's landmark 1912 treatise, 'Express Trusts under the Common Law: A Superior and Distinct Mode of Administration, Distinguished from Partnerships, Contrasted with Corporations', established the classic doctrinal distinction between statutory corporations (which derive their existence from a state legislative franchise) and common-law express trusts (which derive their existence from the natural and common-law right of sui juris individuals to contract and convey property to fiduciaries in equity). Under Chandler's doctrine, when Trustees hold full legal title and plenary management control without partner-style control by beneficiaries, the arrangement is a true trust in equity rather than a partnership or corporate franchise.
